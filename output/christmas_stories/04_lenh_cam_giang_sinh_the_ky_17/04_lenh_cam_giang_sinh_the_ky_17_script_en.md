Try to imagine a bitterly cold December 25 morning in the heart of England in the mid-seventeenth century. Fog blankets the ancient cobblestone streets. Yet from church steeples across the land, not a single bell rings out. There are no candles glowing behind windowpanes, and no wreaths of ivy or sprigs of holly hanging by the front doors.

Instead, the hobnailed boots of soldiers strike steadily against the damp pavement. They stop along every row of merchant stalls to check whether bakeries, cloth shops, and blacksmiths are open for business. If a tradesman dares to close his shop to stay home with his wife and children, he is breaking the law. If the rich aroma of roasted meat or spiced mince pies drifts out from a small kitchen, soldiers have the authority to burst in, seize the food, and impose a fine.

It sounds like a work of fiction, yet it is a historical reality that lasted for decades. What shocks most people today is not just that Christmas was outlawed, but who passed the ban. They were not enemies of Christianity. They were deeply devout Christians who read the Bible every day and believed they were defending the purity of the faith.

Why did believers in Christ pass laws forbidding the celebration of Christ's birth? What happened when ordinary people rose up to defend their Christmas Eve? And why did that ban cross the Atlantic Ocean, lasting twenty-two years in colonial America?

To understand this paradox, we have to travel back to England in the 1640s, when the entire kingdom was engulfed in the flames of the English Civil War.

At that time, England was torn in two between King Charles I and the English Parliament. Within Parliament's ranks, a strict Protestant reform movement was steadily gaining control. They were the Puritans.

The Puritans longed to cleanse the Church of England of every trace of what they viewed as remnants of Roman Catholicism. To them, true faith had to follow Scripture strictly, neither adding nor removing any ritual or holiday invented by human hands.

From that theological stance, Christmas became a prime target for two very clear reasons. First, Puritan scholars pointed out that the New Testament never records December 25 as the day Jesus was born, nor does it command believers to celebrate an annual feast of his birth. In their view, the only holy day ordained by God was the weekly Sabbath on Sunday.

Second, and what outraged them most, was what actually happened on the streets of England during the Middle Ages and the Tudor era. Christmas back then looked nothing like the cozy, peaceful family gathering we picture today. Stretching across twelve days in the dead of winter when farm work paused, the festival often spiraled into heavy drinking, all-night gambling, extravagant feasting, and street rowdiness. Many towns even appointed a Lord of Misrule to turn normal social order upside down.

Looking at that spectacle, the Puritans asked a question that was not easy to dismiss: how could anyone honor a holy Savior through drunkenness and excess? To them, Christmas had degenerated into an excuse for moral decay.

As Parliament's armies gained the upper hand in the Civil War, the Puritans began turning their convictions into law.

The first blow fell in December 1644. That year, December 25 landed squarely on the last Wednesday of the month, which Parliament had already designated as a regular wartime day of fasting. After heated debate, the Long Parliament issued an ordinance turning Christmas Day itself into a solemn day of fasting and repentance. Instead of feasting, the public was ordered to abstain and repent of their sins, including the sin of having reveled too freely during Christmases past.

In early 1645, Parliament went further, issuing the Directory for Public Worship to replace the traditional Book of Common Prayer, officially stripping all major holy days from the church calendar.

Yet erasing a centuries-old tradition proved far from easy. Over the next two winters, many shops still shut their doors and many churches quietly held services. Losing patience with this quiet defiance, Parliament decided to act decisively.

On June 8, 1647, the English Parliament passed an act known as the Ordinance for Abolishing of Festivals. The law declared Christmas, Easter, and Whitsuntide completely abolished. Every shop was required to stay open for normal trade, while churches were forbidden from holding services to mark the day. To appease apprentices and servants who had just lost one of their rare holidays, Parliament designated the second Tuesday of every month as a secular day of recreation instead.

Yet for millions of ordinary people, a soulless bureaucratic Tuesday could never replace Christmas Eve. To them, it was a rare moment in the freezing winter for neighbors to share a warm slice of pie, for the poor to receive a small gift, and for families to gather and remember that light had entered the darkness.

When the winter of 1647 arrived, tension across England was stretched like a taut wire. In London, authorities had to dispatch officers to tear down sprigs of holly and ivy that residents had deliberately hung on market gates overnight.

And then, public fury erupted most violently in the ancient city of Canterbury in the county of Kent on the morning of December 25, 1647.

Under Parliament's orders, city officials proclaimed that every shop had to open for business. The Mayor of Canterbury, William Bridge, personally led officials and halberd-bearing guards on patrol through the streets. Finding many shops still tightly shuttered and clusters of townsfolk dressed in their finest clothes, Mayor Bridge shouted threats to arrest anyone who disobeyed and throw them into the stocks. When one shopkeeper loyal to the government opened his stall and laid out his wares, the crowd immediately jeered and hurled objects at his counter.

In an instant, all restraint snapped. An angry crowd surrounded the patrol and shoved Mayor William Bridge sprawling into the muddy street. After driving the officials back, the people of Canterbury brought inflated pig-bladder balls out into the main street, and hundreds plunged into a chaotic street football match that raged for hours. Holly branches were hung across doorways, the windows of staunch Puritans were smashed, and the crowd roared their rallying cry: For God, King Charles, and Kent.

Later historians gave the uprising a name both humorous and poignant: the Plum Pudding Riots. Because what the people were defending was not only church liturgy, but also their Christmas pudding and the simple warmth of winter joy.

Yet the upheaval in Canterbury carried heavy consequences. When Parliament sent troops to suppress the city and prosecute the rioters, waves of resentment spread across Kent, helping spark the Second English Civil War and the bloody Battle of Maidstone in 1648.

In the end, Parliament still prevailed. In 1649, King Charles I was executed and England entered the Commonwealth period. By 1653, the powerful general Oliver Cromwell became Lord Protector at the head of the nation.

Today, many people call Oliver Cromwell the man who stole Christmas. Strictly speaking according to historical records, it was the Long Parliament that enacted the bans of 1644 and 1647, before Cromwell rose to supreme power. However, under Lord Protector Oliver Cromwell, the ban remained in force and in several years was enforced even more harshly.

In December 1652, Parliament reiterated the prohibition and sent soldiers patrolling the marketplaces. Contemporary accounts describe troops even inspecting kitchens to confiscate roasting meat prepared for forbidden feasts. By December 1656, hardline lawmakers deliberately forced Parliament to sit in session on December 25 itself, taking note of anyone who dared stay home to celebrate.

Yet the most vivid and haunting portrait of this era survives in the diary of the English writer John Evelyn.

On the morning of December 25, 1657, John Evelyn and his wife quietly attended a private chapel inside Exeter House near the Strand in London, where the clergyman Peter Gunning was secretly conducting a Christmas service for a small congregation.

Just as the sermon ended and worshipers began stepping forward to the altar for Communion, heavy footsteps thundered outside. Soldiers burst in and surrounded the chapel, holding the entire congregation like prisoners. In his diary, John Evelyn recorded a detail that still stills the heart: as believers stepped forward to receive the consecrated bread and wine, soldiers aimed their muskets straight at them, as if ready to open fire right in front of the altar. Thankfully, having no specific orders to disrupt the sacrament itself, the soldiers waited until the service concluded before arresting and questioning everyone one by one, eventually releasing them.

In the capital of a Christian nation, believers had to celebrate the birth of Christ staring down the barrels of muskets. And yet Christmas did not die. It retreated behind closed doors, surviving in secret family meals and underground pamphlets printed in defense of the feast.

Then history turned once more. In September 1658, Lord Protector Oliver Cromwell died. The Commonwealth swiftly fractured under the weight of public exhaustion. In May 1660, King Charles II was invited back to take the throne in the Restoration of the monarchy. Every wartime ordinance passed without royal assent was immediately annulled. After thirteen years of prohibition since 1647, Christmas bells rang out across England once again in the winter of 1660.

Yet the story was not over. Because at the very moment England opened its doors to welcome Christmas home, across the Atlantic Ocean an even more enduring ban had just officially begun.

In New England, in what is now the northeastern United States, the earliest settlers of Plymouth Colony and Massachusetts Bay Colony were largely Puritans who had sailed across the sea to build a society governed by strict biblical ideals. They brought their rejection of Christmas with them.

As early as December 25, 1621, in Plymouth, Governor William Bradford recorded a memorable clash. When he called the settlement out to work, a group of newer arrivals refused, claiming that working on Christmas Day went against their conscience. Bradford excused them for the morning. By noon, however, he caught that very same group gathered in the street pitching the bar and playing stoolball. He immediately confiscated their game equipment and told them bluntly that if working that day went against their conscience, then watching them play while others labored went against his conscience.

In the decades that followed, as more non-Puritans arrived in Boston bringing their end-of-year festivities, colonial authorities turned to the law to stop them.

On May 11, 1659, the General Court of the Massachusetts Bay Colony passed a law outlawing the celebration of Christmas. Anyone caught refraining from work, feasting, or observing the day in any manner faced a fine of five shillings paid to the county treasury. Five shillings in that era was equivalent to several hard days of wages for an ordinary laborer. Simply closing your shop to sit down for a special meal on December 25 made you a lawbreaker in the heart of Boston.

While England repealed its ban just one year later, Boston's law endured for a full twenty-two years, from 1659 to 1681. For twenty-two consecutive winters, no bells rang, courts remained in session, workshops kept their fires burning, and town criers walked the streets reminding residents not to gather.

It was not until 1681, under pressure from King Charles II and the English Crown demanding religious freedom for Anglicans, that Massachusetts was finally forced to repeal the five-shilling fine.

Yet laws can change with a single stroke of a pen, while cultural prejudice takes a century to thaw. For generations after 1681, Christmas was still met with cold disapproval in Boston. Well into the nineteenth century, shops and public schools in Boston stayed open on December 25; students who dared skip class to celebrate risked severe discipline, even expulsion.

Not until 1856 did the state of Massachusetts recognize Christmas as a state holiday. And it was not until June 28, 1870, more than two centuries after Boston's five-shilling law, that President Ulysses S. Grant signed legislation officially establishing Christmas as a federal holiday of the United States.

Looking back across that historical journey spanning both shores of the Atlantic, what invites the deepest reflection is not standing here today to pass judgment on the Puritans of old. They were wrong to use state power, fines, and muskets to coerce human conscience. Yet at the same time, their original concern was not entirely baseless: they feared that noise, drunkenness, and vanity would overshadow the sacred meaning of the day the Savior came into the world. That worry from nearly four centuries ago still lingers as a quiet reminder for our own hurried and commercialized Christmas seasons today.

Still, the deepest legacy left behind by those years of prohibition is the remarkable resilience of hope. Thirteen years outlawed in England and twenty-two years fined in Boston pushed ordinary people to the limit. Earthly power could lock church doors, silence bronze bells, rip down ivy, and aim muskets at an altar. Yet the moment the door of the law cracked open, human hearts lit candles and began to sing again.

Because deep within the human heart, during the darkest and coldest days of winter, there lives a longing that nothing can extinguish: the longing to believe that light is stronger than darkness, the longing to remember that God stepped into history in the form of a tiny child, and the longing to set aside our weariness and sit together in love and forgiveness.

The law can strike a holiday from the calendar, but it can never forbid the human heart from remembering love and belonging.

Whenever you hear Christmas bells ringing through the winter night today, remember that there was once a time when, just to keep that simple warmth alive, generations before us walked quietly through some of the longest winters in history.
