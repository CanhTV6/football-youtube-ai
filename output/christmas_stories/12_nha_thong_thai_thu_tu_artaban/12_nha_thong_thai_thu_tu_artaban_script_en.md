For two thousand years, whenever people speak of Christmas night, everyone pictures the wise men from the East riding camels across the desert, bearing gold, frankincense, and myrrh to worship the Christ Child in Bethlehem.

Yet in 1895, the American author Henry van Dyke wrote a classic work of literature that has moved millions of readers around the world to tears for more than a century. It should be made clear from the very beginning that this is not a story recorded in the Bible, but a deeply humane work of fiction inspired by the Gospel of Matthew. The story poses a beautiful question: what if, long ago, there had been a fourth wise man who also saw the strange star in the sky, who also sold all his possessions to set out in search of the Heavenly King, yet arrived late for thirty-three long years?

His name was Artaban. And what seemed to be the most unfinished, failed journey of his life led to one of the most moving endings ever written in Christmas literature.

The story begins in the ancient mountains of Persia, where sages gazed night after night into the starry sky, searching for signs from above. Artaban was a wealthy, learned man with a generous heart. Through ancient prophetic scrolls and patient astronomical calculations, he and his three closest friends recognized a great portent: a new star was about to appear in the western sky, heralding a divine King soon to be born in the land of Judea.

The four friends agreed on a meeting place at an ancient temple outside the city of Babylon. Whoever arrived first would wait for the others, and when the star blazed forth, all four would cross the desert together toward Jerusalem.

To prepare gifts worthy of the newborn King, Artaban sold his grand house, his land, and all the most precious wealth of his life. In exchange for everything he owned, he bought just three of the rarest jewels on earth. A sapphire as deep blue as the night sky. A ruby as crimson as the dawn. And a pearl as pure white and gentle as summer moonlight.

He tucked the three jewels safely into a pouch inside his cloak, mounted his strongest horse, and galloped alone through the night toward Babylon to meet his three friends in time.

The journey across vast hills and plains stretched on for days and nights without rest. Artaban slept only a few brief hours before spurring his horse onward, knowing that even a single night's delay would force the caravan to leave Babylon before the harsh dry season arrived. At last, on a pitch-black night just a few hours' ride from the meeting place, his horse suddenly halted beside a ruined garden along the road.

Beneath the dark shadow of a date palm, a human figure lay motionless across the path. Artaban dismounted and knelt close to look. It was a gaunt Jewish exile, burning with a deadly swamp fever, his breathing so faint he seemed only a hair's breadth from death.

Artaban lifted his eyes to the sky. The appointed star had already begun to rise high. The hour of gathering at the ancient temple near Babylon was now measured in mere hours. A fierce struggle immediately tore at his heart. If he abandoned this stranger in the cold night and rode on, the man would surely be dead by morning. Yet if he stopped to save him, he would miss the rendezvous, lose his three friends' caravan, and perhaps lose his one and only chance in life to meet the Savior.

Before him lay the choice between journeying to find God in a distant land, or stooping down to save a dying human being right at his feet.

Artaban drew a deep breath and knelt beside the sick man. He took his water flask to cool the man's burning brow, mixed precious herbs from his pack for him to drink, and kept vigil for hours through the night until the fever gradually broke and the man opened his eyes.

When the Jewish exile regained his strength and heard Artaban explain that he was hurrying to find the newborn King of the Jews, the man tremblingly shared a priceless clue from the ancient prophets: that King would not be born in the grand capital of Jerusalem, but in a tiny village named Bethlehem.

Before parting, seeing that the sick man was still too weak and had no money to buy food or hire a donkey to return home, Artaban quietly reached inside his cloak. He drew out the deep blue sapphire, the very first gift he had set aside for the Heavenly King, and placed it into the stranger's hands.

When Artaban spurred his horse at full gallop to the ancient temple outside Babylon, daylight had already broken. The meeting place stood empty, without a single soul in sight. Only a small note lay beneath a stone, left behind by his three friends: they had waited for him until the very last moment, but could delay no longer and had set off across the desert ahead of him.

The first jewel was gone. His companions had departed. With only a single horse, Artaban could never cross the vast desert swept by sandstorms. He had no choice but to turn back to Babylon, using the little money he had left to buy a small train of camels and provisions so he could follow alone toward Bethlehem.

Many grueling weeks passed beneath the scorching desert sun. At last, Artaban set foot on the narrow stone streets of the little village of Bethlehem. Yet the village that day felt strangely quiet. There were no cheers, no bustling crowds.

He knocked at the door of a modest house on the edge of the village, where a young mother was rocking her baby son to sleep in her arms. When Artaban asked about the three travelers from the East and the newborn Child, the woman sighed and told him that indeed, three days earlier, wise men from the East had come bearing gold, frankincense, and myrrh to worship a child. Yet the very next night, the child's father, a man named Joseph, had hurriedly woken his wife and carried the Child away to Egypt under cover of darkness.

Artaban's heart sank heavily as he listened. For the second time in his life, he had arrived just three days too late.

Yet while he sat quietly at the humble meal the kind young mother had offered him, sudden piercing screams rang out from the village street, followed by the thunder of hooves and the cold clash of swords and spears.

King Herod's soldiers had poured into Bethlehem. Under the king's ruthless decree, they were bursting into every house to slaughter all baby boys two years old and under.

The young mother inside turned pale as death, hastily clutching her baby boy into a dark corner and gently covering his mouth so he would not cry. Yet a brutal pounding already echoed on the door outside. A fierce-faced captain and his squad of soldiers with drawn swords were marching straight toward that simple wooden door.

Without a second's hesitation, Artaban stepped forward and stood blocking the doorway. His calm bearing and noble garments made the captain pause for an instant. Lowering his voice, Artaban drew the second jewel from his pouch: the crimson ruby, glowing like a drop of blood in the afternoon sun.

He told the captain softly that no one was inside the house besides himself, and that this jewel, worth an entire fortune, would be his if he led his soldiers quietly away. The soldier's greedy eyes locked onto the ruby at once. He snatched the gem, signaled his men to pass over the little house, and marched them down another lane.

Inside the darkened room, the young mother fell to her knees, weeping with gratitude for the benefactor who had saved her child's life. Artaban, meanwhile, only gazed quietly up at the sky, his heart weighed down by a vague sorrow. He had told a lie to save a child, and he had just given away the ruby meant for the Heavenly King. Inside his pouch now remained only one single jewel: the pure white pearl.

Refusing to give up, Artaban turned south once more, tracing the footsteps of the Holy Family all the way into the land of Egypt.

And then, not one year, nor five years passed. Thirty-three long years slipped by along the dusty roads from Egypt back through the land of Israel.

The handsome nobleman of Persia had become a bent old man with snow-white hair, leaning his trembling steps on a worn wooden staff. Across those thirty-three years, he passed through countless cities, knocked on the doors of countless villages, and asked everywhere for the King born in Bethlehem. Yet wherever he went, he found only suffering souls in need of a helping hand.

He paused to bind the wounds of lepers cast out by society. He shared half his loaf of bread with orphaned children by the roadside. He visited prisoners forgotten in dark dungeons. The years drifted away in those quiet, humble acts, while the King he sought day and night always seemed one step ahead, forever just beyond his reach.

Only one thing did Artaban keep clutched against his chest for all thirty-three years, even when he himself went hungry and ragged: the final white pearl. He told himself that though he was old and drawing near the end of his days, he must keep that pearl so that at least once before closing his eyes, he might kneel and offer it to the King of his life.

And then, on a spring morning during the feast of Passover, after thirty-three years of searching, the weary old Artaban stepped through the gates of Jerusalem.

The city was in an unusual uproar that day. Crowds surged and shouted, pouring toward the city gate that led out to a hill called Golgotha. Artaban quickly caught a passerby by the sleeve and asked what was happening. The answer made the old man's heart nearly stop. He was told that Roman soldiers were about to crucify two robbers alongside a man from Nazareth, condemned for claiming to be the King of the Jews.

Thirty-three years. The exact age of the Child of Bethlehem long ago.

A sudden, strange strength surged into Artaban's trembling legs. He realized that perhaps he had not arrived too late after all. Perhaps this final pearl had never been kept as a gift for a birth, but had been preserved for this very hour: to ransom the life of the King before the nails were driven into the cross.

Summoning the last of his fading strength, the old man pushed through the crowd, hurrying toward the hill of Golgotha.

Yet right in the middle of that chaotic street, a band of soldiers dragged a young girl in torn clothes past him. Recognizing the familiar garments of a fellow countryman from Persia, the girl broke free, threw her arms around Artaban's knees, and wept, begging him to save her. Her father was a Persian merchant who had fallen into ruin and died in debt; under the cruel law of the time, the creditor had hired soldiers to seize her and sell her into lifelong slavery to settle what was owed.

Artaban stood frozen in the noisy street.

Ahead of him, just up the slope, lay the hill of Golgotha, where the King he had searched for thirty-three years was about to die. At his feet knelt an innocent young girl about to be cast into the abyss of slavery. In his hand rested only one final pearl.

For the third and final time in his life, Artaban stood before the very same question: run onward to meet God, or stop to save one fragile human life right before his eyes?

The old man's wrinkled hands shook violently. A quiet tear rolled down his hollow cheek. He reached inside his cloak and drew out the pure white pearl, its glow strangely tender in the oppressive midday heat. Artaban placed that final jewel into the hands of the creditor's soldier, buying the young girl's freedom.

Now he was completely empty-handed. No sapphire remained. No ruby remained. No pearl remained. His entire thirty-three-year quest seemed to have ended in utter failure.

At that very moment, in the middle of the day, the sky over Jerusalem suddenly grew as dark as midnight. A violent earthquake shook the entire city at the very hour the One crucified on Golgotha breathed his last. Stone walls cracked open, clouds of dust rose into the air, and a heavy tile shook loose from a nearby roof, falling straight onto old Artaban's temple.

He collapsed onto the roadside in the frantic arms of the young girl he had just rescued. The old man's breathing grew fainter with each passing moment. As blood dampened his snow-white hair, Artaban's parched lips moved softly, as if speaking to someone unseen whom the girl beside him could not behold.

In the fading awareness of his final moments, the old man cried out in deep sorrow: Lord, for thirty-three years I have searched for you everywhere, yet I have never seen your face, nor have I ever offered you a single gift. When did I ever see you hungry, or thirsty, or naked, or in prison?

The ruined street suddenly fell into a wondrous stillness. And then a voice, warm and gentle to the very depths of the soul, answered the dying old man, the very words of Christ in the Gospel of St. Matthew:

Truly, I say to you, whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of mine, you did for me.

A radiant smile of peace suddenly lit up Artaban's aged, dust-stained face. He was not late. He had never missed a single appointment. The sapphire given to the sick man by the road to Babylon, the ruby that saved a child on that night in Bethlehem, and the pearl that ransomed a young girl's freedom in the streets of Jerusalem, all of them had gone straight into the hands of the King he had sought his whole life long.

Artaban gently closed his eyes. The thirty-three-year journey of the fourth wise man had finally reached its peaceful harbor.

Though the story of Artaban is a work of literature written more than a hundred years ago, perhaps the reason it still lives in the hearts of millions every Christmas is that it touches the very heart of the Gospel.

So often in life, we are much like Artaban: we lift our eyes searching for grand gestures and dazzling gifts to offer up to heaven, forgetting that God is not waiting for us in some distant palace. He is right here by the roadside, in someone who is sick and longs for a little compassion, in a family struggling through the winter, or in a loved one simply waiting for a gentle word of care.

This Christmas season, you do not need gold, frankincense, or priceless jewels. The very moment you are willing to pause your steps for someone right beside you who needs you, in that very instant, you have already found the King of Christmas night.
