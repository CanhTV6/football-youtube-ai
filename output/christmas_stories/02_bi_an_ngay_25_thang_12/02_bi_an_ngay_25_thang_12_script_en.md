Every year in the final days of December, as winter winds sweep through the streets, billions of people around the world turn toward a familiar date. December 25. Church bells ring out, lights illuminate every window, and nativity scenes of Bethlehem are set up to celebrate the birth of Jesus.

Yet there is a truth that surprises many people. If you open all four Gospels in the Bible and read them carefully from the first page to the last, you will not find a single line recording the day or month Jesus was born. There is no number twenty-five. There is no December. In fact, for more than the first two hundred years, followers of Christ never celebrated Christmas on this day at all.

So where, then, did December 25 actually come from?

Online today, people debate this fiercely. Some insist that Christmas was merely borrowed from an ancient Roman festival worshiping the Sun god. Yet other historians point to a remarkably simple addition calculation that appeared decades before the Roman emperor ever established that Sun festival. Today, let us journey back nearly two thousand years, setting aside dry jargon, to hear the true story behind winter's greatest holiday.

First, let us look into the Bible itself to see if there are any clues. Among the four Gospels, only two, Matthew and Luke, recount the night of Christ's birth. Both mention major historical markers: Jesus was born in the village of Bethlehem, during the reign of King Herod, at the time of a Roman imperial census. From those markers, historians can estimate that the year of Christ's birth fell a few years before the year one on today's calendar, since King Herod had already died a few years earlier. Yet as for which month or day Jesus was born, the Bible remains completely silent.

Many have tried to guess from the detail of shepherds keeping watch in the fields on the night of his birth. Some argue that winter in that region is far too cold for shepherds to be outdoors in December, so Jesus must have been born in spring or autumn. That sounds very reasonable at first. However, when researchers closely examined the climate of the hills around Bethlehem, they discovered that winter there does not bring heavy snow like in Europe. After the autumn rains, the pastures remain green, many nights stay dry, and flocks raised for sacrifices at the Jerusalem Temple were kept grazing outdoors all year round. Therefore, the image of the shepherds neither proves Jesus was born in December nor disproves it.

Even more intriguing is how the followers of Christ lived during the first two centuries. In that era, people rarely celebrated birthdays at all. In ancient Roman society, birthday banquets were often tied to indulgence or the worship of emperors who claimed to be gods. A famous third-century preacher once pointed out that in the entire Bible, only two people ever held birthday feasts, the Pharaoh of Egypt and King Herod, and both banquets ended in bloody beheadings.

For that reason, throughout the first two hundred years, what mattered most to believers was not the day Christ was born, but the day he rose from the dead, Easter. They even regarded the day a martyr died as their true birthday, the day they entered eternal life.

Even so, human curiosity exists in every age. Around the year 200, a scholar living in Egypt recorded that a few groups of believers had grown curious and tried to calculate the exact day Jesus was born. Amusingly, every group came up with a completely different result. Some guessed May 20. Others insisted it had to be in April. Still another group chose January. No one agreed with anyone else. That brief note stands as the clearest proof that even by the end of the second century, the Christian world still had no shared date for Christmas.

It was not until more than a century later, when the Roman Empire ended its persecution of Christianity and churches were allowed to open publicly, that the story turned a new page.

In the mid-fourth century, in the capital city of Rome, a skilled calligrapher crafted a handsome almanac as a gift for a wealthy nobleman. Inside that ancient calendar was a page listing the death anniversaries of Roman martyrs. And right on the very first line, placed with supreme honor above every saint, stood a brief entry written in Latin: December 25, Christ born in Bethlehem of Judea.

When historians closely examined the chronological markers inside that ancient calendar, they discovered the list had actually been copied from an original source dating back to AD 336. In other words, at least by AD 336, the people of Rome were already officially gathering in church to celebrate Christmas on December 25. That is the oldest and most certain trace history has preserved.

Yet the story does not end there. If you left Italy in that era and traveled East, to Egypt or the Middle East, you would witness a completely different scene. In the East, people did not celebrate Christmas in December. They held a major feast on January 6, known as Epiphany, celebrating together the birth of Christ, the visit of the Magi, and Christ's baptism in the Jordan River.

We still preserve a vivid memory of the moment these Eastern and Western traditions met. On December 25, AD 386, in a great city of the Middle East, St. John Chrysostom, one of the most gifted preachers of antiquity, stepped up to the pulpit. Before thousands of still-puzzled parishioners, he openly stated that the December 25 feast had been introduced from the West less than ten years earlier. Many in the church that day were still unsure whether they should adopt this new celebration. Yet with a warm and persuasive voice, St. John Chrysostom called Christmas the mother of all feasts, urging everyone to welcome it with joy.

From that point on, East and West exchanged cultural gifts. The East adopted Rome's December 25 as Christmas. Meanwhile, the West adopted the East's January 6 as Epiphany, honoring the visit of the Magi. Only the ancient Armenian Church preserved the original tradition, continuing to celebrate Christmas on January 6 to this very day.

Hearing all this, you are probably asking the most important question of all: why did Rome choose December 25 in the first place, rather than any other day of the year?

To answer this question, historians over the past century have followed two major clues. And to be completely honest, even today, no one can declare with absolute certainty that only one of them is right. Let us hear both stories, and you will see that each carries its own compelling logic.

The first clue leads us into the bustling streets of ancient Rome in late December.

In an age before electricity, winter in Europe was a long, bitter, and dark stretch of time. Toward the end of December, the days grew ever shorter while the nights seemed endless. Ancient peoples looked up to the sky and eagerly awaited a special turning point: the winter solstice. That was the day when darkness lasted longest in the year, yet immediately afterward, the sun began to linger in the sky a little longer each day, promising that spring would eventually return.

Because of this, from mid-December onward, ancient Romans had long celebrated lively winter festivals. They decorated their homes with evergreen branches, lit bright candles, feasted through the night, and exchanged gifts. In particular, in the late third century, a Roman emperor seeking to unite a fracturing empire built a magnificent temple to a deity called the Unconquered Sun. And on the ancient calendar of that era, the winter solstice, December 25, was regarded as the birthday of the Unconquered Sun.

Against that backdrop, many historians believe the leaders of the Church in Rome made a remarkably wise decision. Rather than forbidding people from rejoicing during the coldest, darkest days of the year, they chose December 25 itself to celebrate the birth of Christ. It was like a confident proclamation in the heart of the empire: the sun in the sky is merely a light created by the Maker, while Jesus Christ is the true Sun of Righteousness, the Light that never fades. Notably, in that very Roman calendar of AD 354 we mentioned earlier, December 25 lists both the birth of Christ and the festival of the Unconquered Sun.

This story sounds clear, natural, and convincing. For decades, nearly everyone assumed that Christmas simply borrowed its date from the Sun god's festival.

Yet when scholars dug deeper into ancient manuscripts, they stumbled upon a detail that overturned that assumption. As it turned out, Christians during the era of persecution fiercely avoided pagan festivals. Many early Church leaders openly criticized Rome's noisy winter revelries. Even more importantly, traces of December 25 appear in Christian writings from the early third century, decades before that Roman emperor ever built the temple of the Unconquered Sun.

If they were not copying the Sun festival, where did they get December 25? This brings us to the second clue, an even more surprising story: the story of adding nine months.

Ancient Jews and early Christians shared a deeply poetic belief. They believed that God's greatest figures lived an exact, complete number of years, with not a day too many or too few. Therefore, the day their life began and the day they departed this world would fall on the exact same date on the calendar. Applying that belief to Jesus, ancient Christians believed that the day he was conceived in Mary's womb must match the exact day he was crucified on the cross.

So on what day was Jesus crucified? According to the ancient Jewish calendar, it was the fourteenth day of the spring month before Passover. Around the year 200, scholars in North Africa and Rome tried converting that date onto the familiar solar calendar. And they calculated it to be March 25. Remarkably, ancient thinkers also believed that March 25, right at the spring equinox when day and night are equal in length, was the very first day God created heaven and earth. To the ancient mind, nothing could be more fitting than the day the world was created, the day Mary consented to conceive Jesus, and the day Christ gave his life to redeem humanity all converging on a single date: March 25.

Now, try a very simple piece of arithmetic. If March 25 is the Feast of the Annunciation, the day Mary conceived Jesus, then adding a full nine months of a mother's pregnancy, what exact date does the child's birth fall on?

March 25 plus nine months. The result lands to the very day: December 25.

What makes historians nod in admiration at this calculation is that it does not only explain the West, it also neatly unlocks January 6 in the East. For when Eastern scholars used a different ancient Greek calendar to convert the day of Christ's crucifixion, they did not arrive at March 25, but at April 6. And try adding nine months to April 6. It lands squarely on January 6. One simple calculation born within Christian belief itself unlocked both the Eastern and Western mysteries at the very same time.

After hearing both clues, you may wonder: in the end, which story is true?

Many historians today suggest that the two stories do not actually contradict each other, but instead complement one another in a remarkable way. It is quite possible that the nine-month calculation from March 25 first planted the seed of December 25 very early within the Christian community. Then, in the fourth century, when the Church stepped into the open and celebrated the feast publicly in the heart of Rome, the coincidence with the winter solstice and the festival of the Unconquered Sun became a golden opportunity. It allowed early believers to share their faith through an image anyone gazing at the sky could understand: darkness will always give way to the Light.

In the early fifth century, the wise bishop St. Augustine looked straight at that coincidence without shying away from it. In a Christmas sermon, he reminded his congregation with a smile that we celebrate December 25 not to worship the sun as pagans do, but to worship the One who created the sun. He also pointed out a poetic beauty in nature: starting from the very day of Christ's birth, the long winter nights begin to grow shorter little by little, while the daylight steadily grows stronger.

Looking back across that nearly two-thousand-year journey, there is something deeply meaningful that not everyone knows. The Catholic Church has never required anyone to believe that December 25 is the exact historical birthday of Jesus. In truth, no one on earth knows for certain which date on the calendar saw Mary wrap the Infant in swaddling clothes and lay him in a manger.

Yet when we reflect more deeply, perhaps the silence of Scripture carries a quiet beauty of its own. What changed human history was never a number printed on a wall calendar. What matters most is the faith that God truly stepped into the world, choosing to become a fragile child to dwell among us.

Whether December 25 came from a nine-month calculation linking the manger to the cross, or from a longing to kindle a warm flame in the coldest winter night of ancient Italy, its meaning has remained unchanged for two thousand years.

The winter solstice is when darkness is at its longest and most bitter. Yet at that very darkest hour, light quietly begins its return. And every time December 25 knocks at our door, history gently reminds us of an inextinguishable hope: the Light has come into the deepest night of winter, and the darkness could not overcome it.
