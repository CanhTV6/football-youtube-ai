Every time December arrives, hundreds of millions of Nativity scenes light up across the world. From the grand squares of Europe and ancient cathedrals to the warm corners of living rooms in countless Vietnamese homes, the image of the manger with statues of the Virgin Mary, St. Joseph, the Child Jesus, the shepherds, and the Three Wise Men has become deeply familiar.

Yet there is a historical truth that astonishes many people.

The very first Nativity scene in human history, born on a freezing winter night more than eight hundred years ago on a mountainside in Italy, had no statue of the Child Jesus at all. There was no statue of the Virgin Mary. No statue of St. Joseph. There was not even a single actor dressed up to portray the Holy Family.

Inside the cave that night, there was only a wooden manger filled with dry hay, a real ox, a real donkey, and a gaunt friar wearing a coarse brown habit standing in silence, tears streaming down his face in the flickering torchlight.

How could an empty manger in a remote mountain village ignite a tradition that has endured for eight centuries? And what truly took place on the night of December 24, 1223, in Greccio?

To understand that night, we must return to the late autumn of 1223, in the heart of central Italy.

At that time, St. Francis of Assisi was only in his early forties, yet his body already bore the heavy marks of illness and exhaustion. He was less than three years away from his death. After years of living in absolute poverty, walking from region to region to preach, fasting severely, and suffering grave illness during his journey to the East, the health of the friar of Assisi had visibly declined. His eyes were growing dim from a persistent eye affliction.

A few years earlier, around 1219 to 1220, St. Francis had crossed the sea to Egypt and the Near East during the height of the Crusades. Historians today still debate whether he actually set foot inside the cave of Bethlehem itself, as contemporary records do not detail his exact itinerary in the Holy Land. Yet a longstanding Franciscan tradition holds that the atmosphere of the land where Jesus was born left a profound mark upon his soul. Whatever the case, the earliest sources affirm one thing with certainty: St. Francis's devotion to the mystery of the Word made flesh burned more fiercely than ever.

November 1223 also marked a major turning point. On November 29 of that year, Pope Honorius III officially approved the Rule of the Order of Friars Minor. After a long and arduous journey as the brotherhood grew to thousands of friars across Europe, St. Francis left Rome and sought refuge in the quiet Rieti Valley.

Here stood limestone hills blanketed in oak forests, natural caves carved into the mountain slopes, and a small village named Greccio. He loved Greccio for the simple poverty of its villagers, and because a close nobleman friend of his lived there: Giovanni Velita of Greccio.

Thomas of Celano, who wrote the First Life of St. Francis only about five years after this event, described Giovanni as a man of good reputation, yet even nobler in his life of virtue. He held worldly status in low regard and cherished a special affection and reverence for St. Francis.

As Christmas of 1223 drew near, an unprecedented desire took root in the heart of St. Francis.

In early thirteenth-century Europe, most people were peasants who could not read. The Mass was celebrated entirely in Latin inside solemn stone churches. For many of the poor, the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem felt more like a distant tale in books than a living, tangible reality.

St. Francis wanted to celebrate Christmas in a vivid, living way, so that his fellow friars and the country folk could see with their own mortal eyes the poverty that the Child Jesus had embraced when He came into the world.

Yet he did not act impulsively. The early thirteenth century was a period when the Church was deeply cautious of spontaneous devotional practices that could easily be mistaken for heresy. According to St. Bonaventure in the Major Legend, precisely because he did not want this initiative to be suspected of unauthorized liturgical innovation, St. Francis carefully sought and received the approval of Pope Honorius III before proceeding. He was deeply creative in spirit, yet always remained in full communion and obedience to the Church.

About fifteen days before Christmas, St. Francis sent for Giovanni Velita of Greccio. In the First Life, Thomas of Celano records St. Francis's earnest request: if Giovanni wished to celebrate the coming feast of the Lord's Nativity with him at Greccio, he should go ahead and carefully prepare everything Francis was about to ask.

Then St. Francis spoke plainly of his wish. He wanted to enact the memory of the Child who was born in Bethlehem, to see with his own eyes the hardships of His infant needs, how He was laid in a manger, and how, upon the dry hay, with an ox and a donkey standing by, He rested.

Hearing this request, Giovanni immediately returned to Greccio and prepared everything down to the last instruction.

The place chosen was neither a castle nor the main church in the center of the village. It was a natural cave on a steep mountainside outside Greccio, near the hermitage of the Franciscan friars. Giovanni had a wooden manger built, filled it with dry hay, and brought a real ox and a real donkey to stand beside it.

In the entire scene, there was not a single statue. No wooden or clay figure of the Infant. No statue of the Virgin Mary. No statue of St. Joseph. Nor was there anyone from the village playing the roles of the Holy Family.

Why such a remarkable choice?

In part, because the sight of the ox and the donkey beside the manger was enough to awaken the memory of Scripture. Although the Gospel of Luke does not name these two animals, Christian tradition had for centuries connected the manger of Bethlehem with the prophecy in the Book of the Prophet Isaiah: the ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master's crib, yet My people do not understand. The warm breath of the two gentle creatures in the winter night served as a quiet reminder that even unreasoning beasts recognize their Creator.

Yet the deepest reason for the empty manger lay in one thing erected directly above it.

It was an altar.

Thomas of Celano explicitly records that the altar for celebrating Midnight Mass was placed right over that manger. St. Francis had no intention of staging a theatrical play for entertainment. He wanted to unite the manger of Bethlehem with the altar of the Eucharist.

In ancient Hebrew, Bethlehem means House of Bread. For St. Francis, the Child Jesus did not need to be represented by a lifeless statue, because right above that manger, when the priest celebrated Mass, the consecrated Bread would become the Body of Christ. The true center of the cave at Greccio was Jesus Christ Himself, mystically present in the Sacrament of the Eucharist, just as humble and small as on the night He lay in the manger long ago.

That manger looked empty, yet in truth it held everything.

Fifteen days of preparation passed. At last, the night of December 24, 1223 arrived.

Winter wind swept along the Rieti Valley. The sun sank behind the rocky ridges, leaving behind a pitch-black night and a biting chill. From nearby hermitages, the Franciscan friars came on foot to gather. At the same time, men, women, and children from Greccio and the surrounding countryside left the warmth of their homes. In the words of Thomas of Celano, everyone carried candles and blazing torches.

Lines of peasants, shepherds, and friars climbed the rugged footpaths winding through the dark oak forest. Hundreds of raised torches snaked upward like small rivers of fire flowing up the mountainside, illuminating an entire corner of the winter night sky.

Thomas of Celano wrote that the dark night suddenly became as radiant as day. The whole forest echoed with hymns of praise, and the rocky cliffs along the mountainside resounded with prayers, harmonizing with the joy of the crowd.

When St. Francis arrived at the cave and saw everything prepared just as he had longed for, his worn face shone with a profound joy. Standing before the hay-filled manger, beside the ox and the donkey in the flickering torchlight, the distance of thousands of miles and more than twelve hundred years of history seemed to vanish. That night, Greccio had truly become a new Bethlehem.

Then Midnight Mass began directly over the manger.

A priest stepped forward to celebrate the Mass, for St. Francis was not a priest. Throughout his religious life, out of deep humility before the priesthood, he accepted only the office of deacon. That night, he put on the vestments of a deacon and stepped out before the congregation crowding around the cave and spilling out to the edge of the forest.

Thomas of Celano recounts that St. Francis sang the Christmas Gospel in a voice that was at once strong, sweet, clear, and resonant. In the stillness of the winter woods, there remained only the crackling fire of the torches, the breathing of the two animals, and the singing voice recounting how Mary gave birth to her firstborn son, wrapped Him in swaddling cloths, and laid Him in a manger because there was no room for them in the inn.

After singing the Gospel, St. Francis preached to the crowd.

No academic theological lecture was delivered that night. St. Francis spoke in words as simple and sweet as honey, telling of the birth of the poor King in the little town of Bethlehem.

And then an unforgettable detail occurred, recorded with great care by Thomas of Celano.

Whenever he wished to speak the holy name of Jesus Christ, his heart overflowed with such ardent love that he could not say it in the ordinary way. He tenderly called Him the Babe of Bethlehem. And every time his lips uttered the word Bethlehem, his voice trembled, lingering softly like the bleating of a young lamb.

The chronicler also wrote that whenever he spoke of the Babe of Bethlehem or the holy name of Jesus, his lips seemed to taste an indescribable sweetness. Tears rolled down the gaunt cheeks of the friar. Standing before a rough manger in the cold night, his homily pierced straight into the hearts of those hardworking people. For they knew the cold, they knew the smell of straw and animal stalls, and they knew what it meant to be pushed to the margins. For the first time, many realized that God was not far away on high, but had stepped down right into the heart of their poverty.

During that very Mass at Greccio, an extraordinary event was also recorded in the ancient biographies. To remain faithful to history, we must clearly distinguish between the public event witnessed by the entire crowd and a spiritual vision passed down through the testimony of a single individual.

Thomas of Celano relates that among those present was a virtuous man of deep piety. In the First Life, he does not name this man, though St. Bonaventure later identified him in the Major Legend as Giovanni Velita of Greccio.

According to Thomas of Celano's account, this witness testified that he beheld a wondrous vision: inside the manger lay a tiny child, motionless, as if sunk in a deep sleep. Then he saw St. Francis draw near, bend down, and touch the child, and the infant suddenly awoke as though roused from a long slumber.

What is remarkable is that Thomas of Celano himself did not recount this vision merely to spark curiosity. Immediately afterward, he clearly explained its spiritual meaning: the Child Jesus had long been forgotten, as if asleep in the hearts of countless people, and through the grace of God working through His servant St. Francis, the image of that Child was awakened and brought back to life in their loving memory.

Whether one approaches that testimony through the lens of faith or as a profound interior experience of the nobleman that night, its core meaning remains unchanged: inside the cold cave of Greccio, something that had slumbered for far too long in the human heart truly came awake.

When Midnight Mass drew to a close, the crowd followed the torchlight back to their homes in a warm joy that did not fade in the night mist. Many villagers gathered handfuls of hay from the manger to keep as a sacred relic. Thomas of Celano recorded local reports that sick animals recovered after eating that hay, and that several women experiencing difficult labor gave birth safely. From a historical perspective, these are not verified medical records, yet they stand as clear testimony to how deeply that Christmas night in Greccio touched the faith of ordinary people.

Few on that mountainside suspected they had just witnessed a moment that would reshape the cultural history of the entire world.

As for St. Francis, less than three years later, on the evening of October 3, 1226, he died in Assisi, lying on the bare earth in keeping with the ideal of poverty he had chosen all his life. Yet the flame he lit at Greccio never went out.

Not long after St. Francis was canonized in 1228, a chapel was built right inside that ancient cave, with its altar placed upon the exact spot of the original manger. To this day, pilgrims visiting the sanctuary of Greccio can still step into that narrow hollow in the rock and gaze upon the ancient fresco depicting that first Christmas Mass.

From the Rieti Valley, St. Francis's inspiration followed the footsteps of Franciscan friars across Italy, crossed the Alps, and spread throughout all of Europe.

A little over sixty years after the night at Greccio, around 1289 to 1291, Pope Nicholas IV, the first Franciscan pope, commissioned the sculptor Arnolfo di Cambio to carve a Nativity set in marble for the Basilica of Saint Mary Major in Rome. It remains one of the earliest permanent Nativity sculptures still in existence today.

From then on, the art of crafting Nativity scenes, which Italians call the presepe, flourished vibrantly. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, artisans in Naples elevated the Nativity scene to a pinnacle of craftsmanship, weaving into it the everyday life of bakers, fishmongers, and musicians, as if to say that Jesus was born right in the midst of ordinary humanity. Then the Nativity scene crossed the oceans to the Americas, Asia, and Africa, entering every church and every Vietnamese home each winter.

Nearly eight hundred years later, on December 1, 2019, Pope Francis personally made a pilgrimage to that very cave in Greccio to sign the Apostolic Letter Admirabile Signum, calling the Nativity scene a living Gospel that speaks directly to the human heart across every age.

Today, amid streets dazzling with lights, glittering gifts, and elaborate Nativity displays, the story of Greccio gently draws us back to its earliest roots.

The first Nativity scene had no electric lights, only torches warming the hands of peasants in the freezing night. There were no marble statues or splendid garments, only a cold hollow in the rock, a handful of dry hay, the warm breath of an ox and a donkey, and an altar set directly above an empty wooden manger.

St. Francis did not build the Nativity scene to make Christmas more extravagant. He built it to remind humanity that the Most High chose to enter this world in poverty, humility, and the closest nearness to the smallest among us.

More than eight hundred years have passed. The wooden manger at Greccio has faded into history, and the trembling voice of the friar, bleating like a lamb as he spoke the name Bethlehem, now echoes only in the pages of old chronicles.

Yet the question that the empty manger of 1223 quietly poses to each of us today remains untouched by time: amid all the noise of the holiday season, have we still left a quiet and humble space within our hearts where the Babe of Bethlehem can truly awaken?
