Pause for a minute and ask yourself a very honest question. If you were the Creator of heaven and earth, holding all the power of the universe in your hands, and one day you decided to come down and live among humanity, where would you choose to be born?

By ordinary human reasoning, almost anyone would choose a place worthy of such majesty. A grand marble palace in the capital of Rome, where the most powerful emperor on the planet sits upon his throne. Or a resplendent citadel in the royal city of Jerusalem, guarded by thousands of soldiers, with vaults overflowing with gold and silver to command the world with ease.

Yet more than two thousand years ago, God did something completely contrary to every clever calculation of mankind. He did not choose a palace. He did not choose a throne. He chose to be born in a tiny village belonging to an occupied people. On the very night of his birth, his family could not even find a room at an inn. The first cradle of the Creator was a feeding trough for animals. His parents were so poor that when they went to the Temple, they could afford only two small birds as an offering. And before he could even walk, that Child had to be carried away in the dead of night like a penniless refugee all the way to Egypt.

Why was it so? Why would an almighty God choose for himself a beginning so poor, so difficult, and so full of tears?

That question does not belong to you or me alone. For two thousand years, countless people have stood before the Christmas nativity scene wrestling with the very same wonder. To find the answer, we do not need lofty theories. We need only reopen the real scenes of that night in Bethlehem long ago, and look closely at the way God stepped into human life.

First, look at the two utterly opposing worlds that existed side by side on the first Christmas night recorded in the Gospel of Saint Luke.

At one end of the world stood the magnificent capital of Rome. At the pinnacle of power sat Emperor Caesar Augustus, one of the mightiest rulers in ancient history. With a single stroke of his pen signing a census decree, millions of people across the Mediterranean immediately had to pack their belongings and travel to be registered. Gold and silver coins circulating throughout the empire bore his engraved likeness, hailing him as the son of a god and the bringer of peace to the world.

A little closer, right in the capital city of Jerusalem, ruled the notorious King Herod the Great. He lived in some of the most opulent palaces of his age, built towering fortresses across the desert, and rebuilt the Temple in dazzling white stone. The power and gold in his hands were beyond measure. Yet behind those cold marble walls beat a heart forever trembling with paranoia, so suspicious that he had even his own wife and sons executed for fear of losing his throne.

And now, leave those brightly lit palaces behind and walk down to a small village only about ten kilometers south of Jerusalem: the village of Bethlehem.

That night, there were no brass trumpets to welcome him, no honor guard, and no dignitaries who even knew. Amid the crowds thronging back to their ancestral town to register under the Roman emperor's decree, a poor carpenter named Joseph quietly led his wife Mary, who was about to give birth, in search of a place to rest. Yet every door of every inn was already full.

In the chill of the Judean hills, the One who created every star in the sky cried out his first breath in a humble shelter meant for animals. His mother wrapped him in swaddling clothes with her own hands and laid him in a manger.

The contrast is so vast it almost shocks ordinary thinking. On one side stand earthly kings gripping gold, armies, and grand palaces. On the other lies the Son of God huddled amid cold straw. This was in no way an unintended accident. It was a deliberate choice.

And the first reason for that choice appears right in the very first group of guests who approached the manger that night.

In the Gospel of Saint Luke, when the Infant Jesus was born, the angel of God did not fly to the palace of the Roman emperor. The angel did not knock on the doors of King Herod's fortress, nor invite the wealthy elite of Jerusalem. The very first people on the entire earth to receive the glad tidings from heaven were a group of shepherds keeping watch by night in the freezing fields around Bethlehem.

In Jewish society two thousand years ago, shepherding was not the romantic image we see in paintings today. They were a lowly class who slept out in the open fields all year round, their clothes stained with dust, looked down upon as uneducated, unable to observe all the intricate ritual washings of the law, and their testimony was not even trusted in a court of law. They were people pushed to the margins of society.

Now, try to picture this. If on that night Jesus had been born in a magnificent palace, with spear-bearing guards flanking the grand gates, with towering stone walls and costly velvet carpets, would those shepherds in their tattered clothes have ever dared to come near? Surely not. Even if an angel had brought them the news, the guards at the gate would have driven them away from afar. And they themselves would have shrunk back outside the fence in shame, thinking that so exalted a King could never be meant for poor outcasts like them.

Yet because God chose to lie in a manger inside a shelter for animals, every barrier vanished completely.

A manger has no locked doors. No guards demanding a pass. No grand marble steps to make the poor hesitate before stepping inside. On that night in Bethlehem, empty-handed shepherds with the scent of hay clinging to their cloaks could run straight in, kneel right beside the Infant, and look directly into the face of God without feeling unworthy or turned away.

And what is the wonder of it all? It was not only the poor who could enter that humble place. Not long afterward, learned and wealthy Magi from the distant East, bearing gold, frankincense, and myrrh, also had to stoop low to pass through that humble doorway and bow before the Child. Had the Lord been born in a palace, he would have belonged only to the privileged class. Yet because he chose to be born in poverty, he became the God of everyone, from the lowliest to the wealthiest, leaving not a single soul outside.

Still, the story does not stop on the night of Bethlehem. One might still ask: very well, suppose God chose a poor birth to draw close to the poor, but why did his entire life afterward also have to pass through so much hardship, want, and suffering?

Look back at the tear-stained road that Jesus walked from his very earliest days.

Forty days after his birth, his parents brought him to the Temple in Jerusalem. Under the Law of Moses, families of means offered a lamb, while only families too poor to afford a lamb offered two turtledoves or two young pigeons. And Scripture records that his family offered that modest pair of birds.

Before he could even enjoy a peaceful childhood, King Herod's sword of slaughter descended upon Bethlehem. The Infant Jesus had to flee in the darkness with his parents into Egypt, living the life of a displaced refugee in a foreign land.

When he returned to the land of Israel, he grew up in Nazareth, a village so poor that contemporaries scoffed, asking how anything good could ever come from Nazareth. For thirty long years, the Creator of the entire universe lived quietly in a simple carpenter's workshop, earning his daily bread by the sweat of his brow and calloused hands.

And when he stepped out to preach, his life was no more abundant. Jesus himself once spoke a brief sentence that aches to the very core of the heart: foxes have dens and birds of the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to rest his head. He knew what it meant to be faint with hunger after forty days in the desert. He sat exhausted beside a well under the blazing midday sun, asking a stranger for a drink of water. He wept before the tomb of his close friend Lazarus. He was driven out by the people of his own hometown, betrayed by a close disciple and sold for thirty pieces of silver, abandoned by all his friends in the dark of night, and at last breathed his final breath stripped bare upon a cross.

Why did God have to taste so many of the sorrows of human existence?

Think of a moment in your own life when you fell to the very bottom of despair. A day when you were exhausted beneath the burden of making ends meet. A night when you felt utterly alone because loved ones misunderstood you or turned their backs. An afternoon when you sat in numb silence amid loss, illness, or failure.

In that dark hour, suppose God were only a majestic ruler seated upon a glittering throne high above the clouds, who had never once known hunger, never once known cold, never had a door slammed in his face, never been betrayed or shed a tear. Then, if we lifted our eyes to cry out, a bitter protest would always echo deep within the human heart: Lord, you have always lived in comfort up above; how could you ever understand my pain down here on earth?

That is the second profound reason why God chose to step into the most arduous of lives.

The Letter to the Hebrews in the New Testament expresses this in a sentence that has warmed countless generations: we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, for he has been tested in every way just as we are, yet without sin.

God did not stand at a distance handing down empty words of comfort. He stepped straight down into the mire of the human condition, drinking to the dregs every bitter cup that humanity must drink. He chose to be turned away on his very first night to understand the ache of those with nowhere to belong. He chose to be a refugee in infancy to understand the terror of families forced to flee their homeland. He chose to be a poor laborer earning his bread in sweat to understand the toil of working people. He chose betrayal and agony upon the cross so that no suffering human being on this planet would ever have to feel alone again.

When you shed tears in the dark of night, you are not weeping before a distant, unfeeling deity. You are weeping before a God who has wept real tears, who bears real scars upon his very own hands.

And there is a third reason still, one that turns upside down everything humanity believes about strength and power.

For centuries before Jesus was born, the people of Israel, living under foreign occupation, longed endlessly for a mighty Messiah. In the imagination of most people of that day, that savior-king had to be a commanding general riding a warhorse, wielding a sword to crush the Roman legions and restore a powerful, wealthy kingdom like the days of King David and King Solomon.

If God had truly appeared in that way, bringing boundless gold and overwhelming military might to rule the world, what would have happened? Surely the whole world would have fallen to its knees before him at once. Yet why would they have knelt? Some would have knelt in terror of his power. Others would have followed him craving wealth, titles, and earthly gain. Among all those things, the one thing power and money can never buy is genuine love.

A tyrant like Herod or an emperor like Augustus could use armies to force people to bow their heads, yet they could never force a single person to love them from the heart.

God knows the human heart better than anyone. He does not wish to conquer humanity through fear. He wishes to touch the human heart through love. And so that people might come to him in complete freedom, neither out of fear nor out of ambition for status and wealth, God emptied himself of all dazzling glory, as St. Paul wrote, humbling himself to become a fragile Infant without a single weapon in his hand.

No one can be afraid of a newborn baby lying in a manger. No one approaches a poor family in a stable seeking worldly prestige or profit. Everyone who steps forward to kneel beside the manger of Bethlehem comes for one reason alone: because their heart is genuinely moved by a love willing to humble itself to the very end.

And now, step back two thousand years to see how history itself has answered those two opposing paths of power.

Look at the city of Rome today. The gilded palaces of Emperor Caesar Augustus, who once held the fate of the world in a single signature, are now nothing more than broken brick walls and ruined stone columns exposed to sun and wind for tourists to wander past and admire.

Look at the land of Judea today. The mighty fortresses and lavish palaces of King Herod the Great have crumbled into silent ruins among the desert crags. No one trembles before his wrath anymore.

And what of the poor Child who had doors shut against him on that winter night in Bethlehem?

No army. Not a single gold coin. No palace. Yet for more than two thousand years, his name has crossed every border, every empire, and every language. Billions of people across every continent still recreate that humble manger every December as the most sacred symbol of love and hope. Even all of humanity today, of every faith or of none at all, still counts the days and years on the calendar hanging on the wall from the very moment that poor Child entered history.

The power of swords and gold eventually fades into dust. Only the power of humility and self-giving love endures forever.

Thinking of this, perhaps the question of why Christ was not born into wealth is no longer a puzzling paradox, but becomes the gentlest consolation for each of us this Christmas season.

If this year your life is not as abundant as you had hoped. If the room where you live feels small and cramped, or your heart is still heavy with worry, weariness, and wounds not yet healed. Never think that your life is too ordinary or too messy to be worthy of a true Christmas.

Remember that night in Bethlehem two thousand years ago. God never waited for a magnificent palace or a flawless life before stepping in. The simplest, coldest, and most lacking places were the very ones he freely chose to pause in and bring his light.

For Christmas night is not a story saying that humanity must become perfect before reaching up to heaven. Christmas night is the story that heaven stooped down low, stepping straight into the small struggles of our lives, so that we might know we never have to walk through the winter of this life alone.
